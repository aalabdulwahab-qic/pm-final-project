Problem Hook & Value Proposition

Problem Hook & Value Proposition, Module 1

  • Scenario: Assets InsightHub

Strategic crisis

If Assets InsightHub does nothing for 12 months, every duplicated or ad-hoc request will add another dashboard, extract, or AI use case that users can't easily find or trust. Users will fall back on spreadsheets and manual requests to the Data team. This has three effects:

-Delivery teams stay saturated with repeat requests instead of building reusable products.

  • Leadership receives inconsistent numbers from different sources.
  • Future AI investments are built on a fragmented foundation.

The platform would lose its position as the enterprise source of trusted data and insights.

Moment of misery

A business lead is preparing for a decision meeting and needs a current, reliable figure. They open several dashboards and find slightly different numbers. They search for a report they only think exists, then message the Data team and wait for a manual extract. When the answer arrives, they still aren't sure it's right. The decision is delayed or made on a spreadsheet nobody can verify.

Problem hook

Assets InsightHub already holds trusted enterprise data, but users can't easily find it, understand it, or be sure it's the right answer. Without change, the platform will become a fragmented collection of Data & AI solutions instead of the place where decisions start.

Value proposition

For business users and decision-makers

who need timely, trusted answers to make decisions,

we will provide a single, intelligent way to discover, understand, and use trusted data and insights, so they no longer have to search, request, or verify manually,

because Assets InsightHub already owns the trusted data products, KPIs, and analytics that other sources can't match, and now needs to make them easy to reach and reuse.

Cold-read self-review

Is the business risk high-stakes enough?

Yes, if the risk is framed as a chain: lost trust, then lower adoption, then wasted investment in duplicate and AI efforts. A cold reader will ask for proof, so add one data point, such as repeat-request volume, time to get an answer, or the number of competing versions of a KPI.

Is the misery systemic?

Yes. The problem is not one slow dashboard or one missing report. It is a recurring difficulty in finding, accessing, and trusting the right insight across business needs, and it creates dependency on manual work and the Data team. To support this, use interviews, search logs, or request-ticket themes that show the same pattern across several user groups.

The value is internal: fewer Excel exports and colleague pings, and decisions made on the right number.

If you can estimate it, size it as ___ hours saved per month. Don't invent a revenue number.

Silence carries little risk.

The badge communicates itself. It appears at the moment of choice, so users don't need an announcement to benefit.

If nobody is told, users still see "Official" where they already look. That is low risk compared with a feature that is hidden or needs learning.

The real risks are about quality and owners, not messaging.

Owners. The two owners must know their name will appear and what approving means. If they don't, the popover shows "Approved by" a person who didn't expect it. This is the one place silence costs you.

False or missing badge. A wrong badge damages trust more than no badge. That is a QA and fail-closed problem (E3), not a comms problem.

Over-investing. A multi-channel campaign for a two-metric badge would cost more than the problem is worth and would add noise to the product.

  • Is this a launch or a release? does it need go-to-market, or can it just ship?: It's a release with a small launch layer.

Module 6

Individual Insights

Where the work was hardest, the moment my thinking changed, and what I learned.

Friction points

The hardest part for me was figuring out what really adds value to the product versus what the business needs. The business wanted certified metrics and something to show for governance. But what users needed was simpler: knowing which dashboard they can trust without double-checking it in Excel. Those two overlap, but they aren't the same thing, and I kept asking myself, Am I building this for users, or because it looks good in a plan?

That question changed my choices. I measured whether people actually used the official asset, not how many saw the badge. I sized the launch small, because a big campaign wouldn't have helped users. I also learned that in a data product, trust is the real feature. A certified metric nobody trusts is worth the same as an uncertified one.

The other challenge was planning with missing information. I didn't have owners, dates or real baselines, so I labeled my assumptions instead of inventing numbers.

Next time, I'd confirm the user problem and the accountable owners before designing anything

Aha moment

My aha moment was realizing that shipping isn't success. A certified metric nobody trusts is worth the same as an uncertified one. The real result is changed behavior, like people choosing the official dashboard and using it. Since then I see product management as finding the smallest change that shifts what users do, and then proving it.

Key learnings

The biggest surprise was that a small launch can be the right one. I also learned that my own announcements can spoil an experiment, that trust is the real feature of a data product, and that every metric needs a guardrail and a "what if it goes wrong" plan.
